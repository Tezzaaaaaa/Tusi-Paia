SCRIPTURE · TEXT · LANGUAGE · HISTORY
Read with context

One collection.
Many centuries.

The Bible is a library of writings, not a single book written at one moment. Its books emerged in different communities, languages and historical settings. The collection, its manuscripts and the translations readers use today each have their own histories.

This guide separates historical evidence from religious interpretation. It describes what scholars and collections can document, while recognising that communities understand the sacred meaning of these texts in different ways.

01 / The long view

A timeline of the Bible

Dates are approximate where the evidence does not permit a precise year. These milestones describe composition, preservation, canon and printing — four different processes.

1st MILLENNIUM BCE · APPROXIMATE

Hebrew scriptures take shape

The books later known as the Hebrew Bible were composed and edited over many centuries. They are traditionally grouped as Torah, Prophets and Writings.

3rd–2nd CENTURIES BCE

Greek translation

Jewish translators produced Greek versions of scriptures, commonly called the Septuagint. The collection and contents varied across time and communities.

c. 50–100 CE · APPROXIMATE

New Testament writings

Early Christian letters and narratives were written in Greek. The texts circulated among communities before a widely recognised 27-book New Testament list emerged.

3rd CENTURY BCE – 1st CENTURY CE

Dead Sea Scrolls

Manuscripts found near the Dead Sea include copies of many Hebrew Bible books. They reveal both substantial continuity and textual variants; they are not the original manuscripts of the biblical books.

4th–5th CENTURIES CE

Early complete Christian codices

Large Greek codices such as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus preserve extensive biblical texts. They are handwritten witnesses, not first editions.

c. 382–405 CE

Jerome's Latin Bible

Jerome revised existing Latin translations and translated much of the Old Testament from Hebrew. The Latin Vulgate became highly influential in Western Christianity.

c. 1454–1455

Gutenberg's printed Bible

Johannes Gutenberg's Latin Bible was among the earliest major books printed in Europe with movable metal type. Printing accelerated reproduction; it did not begin the Bible's history.

1526 / 1535

Tyndale's English translations

William Tyndale's New Testament appeared in print in 1526; his English Pentateuch followed in 1530, and parts of the Old Testament appeared later. His work influenced later English Bibles.

1611

King James Version

The English translation commissioned under James I was published in 1611. It drew on earlier English translations and Hebrew and Greek sources; it was not the first English Bible.

02 / The physical record

From scroll to book

The material form changed over time. These simplified drawings are interpretive diagrams, not reproductions of specific artefacts.

Scroll · papyrus or parchment

A long sheet wound around a rod. Ancient Jewish scripture survives in scrolls, including the Dead Sea Scrolls. Papyrus and parchment are different writing materials.

Codex · bound pages

Sheets folded and joined into a book. Codices made it easier to consult different passages and became common among early Christians.

GAGANA SAMOA · SOV

Kenese 1:1

Reading note
The two sentences express the same opening claim, but their word order differs. English begins with “In the beginning”; the Samoan sentence places the action “Na faia” (“created/made”) first and closes with “i le amataga” (“in the beginning”). Translation choices reflect grammar and style, not simply a word-for-word substitution.
04 / The Samoan story

The Bible in Sāmoa

The Samoan Bible emerged through translation, printing, revision and local collaboration. Its history should not be reduced to the work of one missionary or one date.

1830s

Translation work begins

London Missionary Society (LMS) missionaries began translating biblical books into Samoan in the 1830s. The work involved several translators and developed over many years.

1841

Printed portions

A Samoan Gospel of John was printed in 1841, followed by other portions as translation and revision progressed.

1862

First complete Samoan Bible

The Reed Gallery's bibliographic account identifies 1862 as the first complete Samoan Bible, associated chiefly with George Pratt and the LMS translation effort. Some historical accounts give 1860 for the printing and release process; catalogues do not always describe the same edition or stage.

1870s–1884

Revision and a lasting edition

The text was revised over time. A London Missionary Society revision of 1884 became the basis for later reprints, including an 1887 corrected reprint.

2009 → PRESENT

Contemporary Samoan

The Contemporary Samoan New Testament was completed in 2009. Bible Society organisations are working toward a contemporary, meaning-based Old Testament so a full modern-language Bible can be available.

A shared history

Missionary translators were central to the LMS project, but Samoan language knowledge, review and local collaboration were essential to making the text intelligible and usable. Historical accounts differ in how fully they record Samoan contributors.

Why editions matter

“The Samoan Bible” does not refer to only one fixed wording. The 1884 revision, later reprints, the 2009 Contemporary New Testament and the 2017 Samoan Edited Old Version are distinct editorial and translation milestones.

The canon is not identical in every tradition

The Jewish Tanakh contains 24 books. Protestant Old Testaments contain the same books arranged as 39; Catholic Bibles include additional deuterocanonical books, and Eastern Orthodox canons differ again. Most Christian traditions recognise 27 New Testament books. These differences reflect distinct histories of religious communities and canon formation.

What manuscripts can — and cannot — tell us

Manuscripts let scholars compare readings, trace copying and identify variants. They do not give us the physical original of every biblical book, and a manuscript's age alone does not decide which reading is best. Textual criticism weighs multiple witnesses, their relationships and the context of each reading.

\n
Historical care
Dates in older missionary histories, library catalogues and modern Bible-society records can refer to different stages: translation completed, first portion printed, complete edition published, or revised edition released. Where sources differ, this guide states the difference rather than forcing a false single date.
